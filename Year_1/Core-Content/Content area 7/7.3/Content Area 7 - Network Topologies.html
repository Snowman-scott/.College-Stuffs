What is a Network Topology?

  • A network topology is the physical or logical arrangement of devices (nodes) and connections in a computer network
  • It defines how computers, servers, printers, and other devices are connected and communicate with each other
  • Topology affects network performance, reliability, and cost
  • There are two types:
    • Physical topology: The actual layout of cables and devices
    • Logical topology: How data flows through the network

Bus Topology

Overview

All devices are connected to a single central cable (the "bus" or "backbone"). Data travels along the cable in both directions until it reaches its destination.

Single cable (backbone) with devices attached

✓ Advantages

  • Easy to install and extend
  • Requires less cable than other topologies
  • Cost-effective for small networks

✗ Disadvantages

  • If the main cable fails, entire network goes down
  • Performance degrades as more devices are added
  • Difficult to troubleshoot problems

Ring Topology

Overview

Each device is connected to exactly two other devices, forming a circular pathway. Data travels in one direction (or both in a dual-ring) around the ring.

Circular connection - data travels in one direction

✓ Advantages

  • Equal access for all devices
  • Can handle larger networks than bus
  • Performance is predictable

✗ Disadvantages

  • Single device failure can break the network
  • Difficult to add or remove devices
  • Slower than star topology

Star Topology

Overview

All devices are connected to a central hub or switch. All data passes through this central device to reach its destination.

All devices connect to central hub/switch

✓ Advantages

  • Easy to add/remove devices
  • One device failure doesn't affect others
  • Easy to troubleshoot and maintain
  • Better performance than bus or ring

✗ Disadvantages

  • If central hub fails, network goes down
  • Requires more cable than bus
  • More expensive due to hub/switch cost

Mesh Topology

Overview

Each device is connected to multiple (or all) other devices in the network. Can be full mesh (every device connected to every other) or partial mesh (some devices connected to multiple others).

Multiple interconnected paths between devices

✓ Advantages

  • Extremely reliable and fault-tolerant
  • Multiple paths for data transmission
  • No single point of failure
  • High data transfer capacity

✗ Disadvantages

  • Very expensive to implement
  • Complex installation and configuration
  • Requires large amount of cabling
  • Difficult to maintain
6 / 13

Extended Star (Hybrid) Topology

Overview

Multiple star networks are connected together via a central backbone or additional switches. Common in large organisations spanning multiple floors or buildings.

Multiple star networks connected via backbone

✓ Advantages

  • Scalable to large networks
  • Failure in one star doesn't affect others
  • Easy to expand and modify
  • Good for multi-building organisations

✗ Disadvantages

  • More complex than simple star
  • Requires additional switches/routers
  • Higher overall cost
  • Backbone failure affects multiple networks

Wireless Topology

Overview

Devices connect to the network via wireless access points (WAPs) rather than physical cables. Often structured similar to star topology but without physical connections.

Topology Comparison

Key Factors to Consider

Cost: Bus < Ring < Star < Extended Star < Mesh
Reliability: Mesh > Extended Star > Star > Ring > Bus
Performance: Mesh > Star > Extended Star > Ring > Bus
Scalability: Extended Star > Mesh > Star > Ring > Bus
Ease of Installation: Bus > Star > Wireless > Ring > Extended Star > Mesh

Choosing the Right Topology

  • Small networks: Star or Bus
  • Medium businesses: Star or Extended Star
  • Large organisations: Extended Star or Hybrid
  • Mission-critical systems: Mesh or Partial Mesh
  • Temporary/flexible needs: Wireless
