# IT Systems in E-Commerce

## What is E-Commerce?

E-commerce (electronic commerce) is the buying and selling of goods and services over the internet. It encompasses all commercial transactions conducted electronically, including online retail (B2C), business-to-business trade (B2B), consumer-to-consumer marketplaces (C2C), and consumer-to-business models (C2B).

### Types of E-Commerce

- **B2C (Business to Consumer)** - A business sells directly to individual customers (e.g. Amazon, ASOS).
- **B2B (Business to Business)** - Businesses sell products or services to other businesses (e.g. Alibaba, wholesale suppliers).
- **C2C (Consumer to Consumer)** - Individuals sell to other individuals through a platform (e.g. eBay, Depop, Facebook Marketplace).
- **C2B (Consumer to Business)** - Individuals offer products or services to businesses (e.g. freelancers on Fiverr, stock photography sites).

## Advantages of Buying/Shopping Online (for the Customer)

- **Easier price comparison** - Can search across multiple retailers to find the best deals quickly.
- **More convenient** - Shop 24/7 from anywhere with an internet connection, no opening hours.
- **Niche products are easier to find** - Access to specialist retailers worldwide, not limited to local shops.
- **More efficient** - Browse multiple stores in one session without physically travelling between them.
- **No need to travel** - Saves time and money on commuting, parking, fuel etc.
- **Access to customer reviews** - Read real feedback from other buyers before making a purchase decision.
- **Consumer protection** - Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations, online purchases have a 14-day cooling-off period (right to cancel) with no reason required.
- **Wider product range** - Online stores can stock far more products than a physical shop with limited shelf space.
- **Personalised recommendations** - Algorithms suggest products based on browsing and purchase history.

## Drawbacks of Buying/Shopping Online (for the Customer)

- **No physical interaction with the product** - Cannot touch, feel, or try items before purchasing.
- **Security concerns** - Risk of fraud, phishing, data breaches, and identity theft when entering payment details online.
- **Delivery delays and costs** - Items may take days to arrive and shipping fees can add to the total cost.
- **Returns can be inconvenient** - May need to repackage and post items back, sometimes at the customer's expense.
- **Misleading product listings** - Photos and descriptions may not accurately represent the actual product.
- **No personal interaction with staff** - Cannot get face-to-face advice or recommendations from knowledgeable salespeople.
- **Reliance on internet access** - Requires a stable connection; not accessible during outages.
- **Digital exclusion** - Not everyone has the skills or access to shop online (e.g. some elderly users).

---

## Benefits to the Business

- **Increased customer reach** - Can sell to customers nationally and internationally, not limited by geography.
- **Lower overhead costs** - No need to pay rent, utilities, or staffing for a physical storefront.
- **24/7 availability** - The store is always open, generating sales outside of normal business hours.
- **Increased sales and revenue** - Access to a larger market leads to more potential sales.
- **Data collection and analytics** - Can track customer behaviour, preferences, and buying patterns to inform marketing and stock decisions.
- **Increased brand awareness** - Online presence through SEO, social media, and digital marketing reaches a wider audience.
- **Scalability** - Easier to scale up operations without the constraints of physical space.
- **Lower marketing costs** - Digital advertising (e.g. social media ads, email campaigns) can be cheaper and more targeted than traditional methods.
- **Automated processes** - Order processing, stock management, and customer communication can be automated, reducing labour costs.

## Drawbacks to the Business

- **Warehouse and fulfilment management** - Need to manage storage, packing, and dispatch efficiently, which can be complex and costly.
- **Inventory control challenges** - Keeping accurate stock levels across multiple channels is difficult; overselling or stockouts can occur.
- **Supply chain complexities** - Reliance on suppliers and couriers introduces risk of delays and disruptions.
- **High competition** - Low barriers to entry mean many competitors, often leading to price wars and reduced margins.
- **Website maintenance and costs** - Must invest in hosting, security, updates, and potentially development staff.
- **Cyber security threats** - Risk of hacking, data breaches, DDoS attacks; businesses must comply with GDPR and invest in security measures.
- **Customer trust** - Harder to build trust without face-to-face interaction; negative reviews can spread quickly.
- **Returns and refunds** - Online businesses typically experience higher return rates than physical stores.
- **Dependence on technology** - Server downtime, payment gateway issues, or website bugs directly stop sales.

## Key IT Systems Used in E-Commerce

### Website / Online Storefront
The core platform where products are listed and transactions take place. Can be custom-built or use platforms like Shopify, WooCommerce, or Magento. Must be responsive (works on mobile), fast, and user-friendly.

### Payment Processing Systems
Secure systems that handle online transactions. Examples include Stripe, PayPal, and WorldPay. Must use encryption (SSL/TLS) and comply with PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) to protect card details.

### Database Management Systems (DBMS)
Store and manage product information, customer records, order history, and stock levels. Relational databases (e.g. MySQL, PostgreSQL) are commonly used to link related data such as customers to their orders.

### Customer Relationship Management (CRM)
Software that tracks customer interactions, purchase history, and preferences. Helps businesses personalise marketing, manage support queries, and improve customer retention. Examples: Salesforce, HubSpot.

### Stock/Inventory Management Systems
Track stock levels in real time, automate reordering when stock falls below a threshold, and synchronise inventory across multiple sales channels (website, marketplace, physical store).

### Electronic Point of Sale (EPOS)
Used by businesses that also have physical stores. Integrates with the online system to keep stock levels and sales data synchronised across both channels.

### Analytics and Reporting Tools
Track website traffic, conversion rates, popular products, and customer demographics. Examples: Google Analytics. Data-driven decisions help optimise the business.

### Security Systems
- **SSL/TLS certificates** - Encrypt data transmitted between the customer's browser and the server.
- **Firewalls and intrusion detection** - Protect against unauthorised access.
- **Two-factor authentication (2FA)** - Adds an extra layer of security for customer and admin accounts.
- **GDPR compliance tools** - Manage consent, data access requests, and data retention policies.

### Order Management and Logistics
Systems that track orders from placement through to delivery. May integrate with courier APIs (e.g. Royal Mail, DPD) to provide real-time tracking information to customers.

## Legislation Relevant to E-Commerce

- **GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)** - Governs how businesses collect, store, and process personal data. Customers must give consent, and businesses must protect data and report breaches.
- **Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013** - Gives online customers the right to cancel orders within 14 days and receive a full refund.
- **Consumer Rights Act 2015** - Goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose, and as described. Applies to digital content as well.
- **Computer Misuse Act 1990** - Makes unauthorised access to computer systems illegal, relevant to cyber security.
- **PCI DSS** - Industry standard requiring businesses that handle card payments to maintain secure systems and processes.

## Brick and Click Businesses

A brick-and-click business is a company that operates both a physical high-street presence (brick-and-mortar) and an online store (click). This hybrid model gives customers the choice of shopping in-store or online, combining the benefits of both channels.

**Examples:** Argos, John Lewis, Currys, Next, Tesco.

**Advantages of Brick and Click:**
- Customers can browse online and collect in-store (click and collect), or try in-store and order online.
- Wider customer reach than purely physical shops, while maintaining the trust and experience of a physical store.
- Returns are easier as customers can bring items back to a physical location.
- Brand presence in both the digital and physical space increases visibility.

**Disadvantages of Brick and Click:**
- Higher operating costs than a purely online business (rent, staff, utilities for physical stores plus website maintenance).
- Need to synchronise stock and pricing across both channels, requiring integrated IT systems.
- More complex logistics and supply chain management.

## Profile of a Successful Online Retailer: Amazon

### Why Amazon is Successful

Amazon is one of the most successful e-commerce businesses in the world. Key reasons for its success include:

- **Vast product range** - Amazon sells hundreds of millions of products across virtually every category, acting as both a retailer and a marketplace for third-party sellers.
- **Customer-centric approach** - Amazon's mission is to be "Earth's most customer-centric company." Features like one-click ordering, easy returns, and customer reviews all prioritise the buyer's experience.
- **Prime membership** - Amazon Prime bundles fast delivery (often next-day or same-day), streaming video, music, and other perks into one subscription, creating strong customer loyalty and repeat purchases.
- **Recommendation algorithms** - Amazon uses sophisticated AI and machine learning to suggest products based on browsing history, purchase history, and what similar customers bought ("customers who bought this also bought..."). This drives a significant proportion of their sales.
- **Fulfilment network** - Amazon operates massive warehouse and distribution networks, enabling fast and reliable delivery. Their Fulfilment by Amazon (FBA) service also handles storage and shipping for third-party sellers.
- **Competitive pricing** - Dynamic pricing algorithms constantly adjust prices to remain competitive, and the marketplace model drives competition between sellers.
- **Innovation** - Amazon continuously invests in new technology such as drone delivery, cashierless stores (Amazon Go), and voice-activated shopping via Alexa.

### Customer Data Amazon Collects and Uses

Amazon collects extensive data about its customers, including:

- **Personal information** - Name, email address, delivery addresses, phone numbers, payment details.
- **Purchase history** - Every item bought, when it was bought, and how much was paid.
- **Browsing and search data** - Products viewed, search queries entered, time spent on pages, items added to wishlists or baskets.
- **Device and location data** - Type of device used, IP address, approximate location, and with Alexa devices, voice recordings and commands.
- **Streaming habits** - What content is watched on Prime Video, listened to on Amazon Music, and read on Kindle, including duration and frequency.
- **Review and rating data** - Products reviewed, ratings given, and questions asked.

**How Amazon uses this data:**
- **Personalised recommendations** - Algorithms analyse purchase and browsing patterns to suggest relevant products.
- **Targeted advertising** - Amazon groups users into audience segments based on interests and behaviour to serve relevant ads both on Amazon and across the web.
- **Inventory and pricing decisions** - Data on purchasing trends helps Amazon forecast demand, manage stock levels, and set dynamic prices.
- **Improving services** - Customer feedback and usage data informs improvements to the platform, delivery logistics, and new features.

### Cyber Security Risks Faced by Amazon and Its Customers

**Risks to customers:**
- **Phishing attacks** - Criminals send fake emails or texts impersonating Amazon (e.g. fake Prime renewal notices) to trick customers into entering login or payment details on fraudulent websites.
- **Account takeover** - If credentials are compromised (e.g. through data breaches on other sites where the same password was used), attackers can access Amazon accounts, make purchases, or steal personal data. Account takeover attacks rose 141% between 2021 and 2025.
- **Third-party data breaches** - In 2024, a logistics partner (HipShipper) suffered a breach exposing 14.3 million shipping records including customer names, addresses, and phone numbers. Amazon itself was not breached, but customer data was still exposed.
- **Identity theft** - Stolen personal and financial data can be used for fraud.

**Risks to Amazon:**
- **Reputational damage** - Any data breach or security incident erodes customer trust.
- **Financial losses** - Fraud, chargebacks, and the cost of responding to security incidents.
- **Regulatory penalties** - Failure to protect data adequately can result in fines under GDPR (up to 4% of global annual turnover).
- **Ransomware and infrastructure attacks** - In 2025, a ransomware group targeted AWS users by exploiting compromised credentials to encrypt data stored in Amazon S3 buckets.

## Impact of Streaming Services on UK TV/Film Viewing (Past 15 Years)

Streaming services have fundamentally transformed how people in the UK watch television and film:

- **Massive adoption** - Over 20 million UK households now have at least one streaming subscription, up from around 11 million in 2018. The average UK household subscribes to 2-3 streaming services.
- **Decline of traditional broadcast TV** - Viewing of traditional broadcast television (BBC, ITV, Channel 4, Channel 5) has seen its sharpest drop since records began. Average daily broadcast TV viewing fell from around 4 hours in 2010 to 2 hours 38 minutes by 2022, a decline of over 30%.
- **Fewer mass-audience programmes** - The number of programmes attracting over 4 million viewers halved from 2,490 in 2014 to just 1,184 in 2022, as audiences fragment across multiple platforms.
- **On-demand viewing habits** - Viewers increasingly expect to watch what they want, when they want. "Binge-watching" entire series in one sitting has become normalised, changing how content is produced and released.
- **Shift away from scheduled programming** - Younger audiences in particular have largely abandoned traditional TV schedules in favour of on-demand content. 16-24 year olds watch less than one hour of broadcast TV per day on average.
- **Rise of original content** - Streaming platforms invest billions in original programming, creating high-quality content that competes with (and often surpasses) traditional broadcasters.
- **Cord-cutting** - Some households have cancelled traditional TV packages (e.g. Sky, Virgin Media) entirely in favour of cheaper streaming alternatives, though many still combine both.
- **Financial impact** - Consumer spending on digital video in the UK reached over £4.7 billion in 2023, showing the massive economic shift from traditional broadcasting to digital services.
- **Impact on cinemas** - Some films now release simultaneously in cinemas and on streaming platforms, or have shorter theatrical windows, changing the cinema industry.

## Impact of Music Streaming Services on the Music Industry

### Positive Impacts

- **Accessibility** - Streaming has made music more accessible than ever. For a single monthly fee (or free with ads), listeners can access virtually all recorded music ever made, rather than buying individual albums or singles.
- **Reduced piracy** - Streaming largely eliminated illegal downloading. When music is cheap, convenient, and instant, there is little incentive to pirate it. Services like Spotify were explicitly designed to combat piracy.
- **Revenue recovery** - The UK music industry generates over £1 billion annually from streaming, with 114 billion streams in 2024. After years of decline due to piracy and falling CD sales, streaming has driven industry revenue back up.
- **Discovery of new artists** - Algorithm-driven playlists and recommendations expose listeners to new music they might never have found otherwise. Independent artists can upload music directly to platforms without needing a record label.
- **Global reach** - An artist in the UK can instantly reach listeners worldwide, removing geographic barriers to distribution.
- **Data and insights** - Artists and labels can see detailed analytics on who is listening, where, and how often, helping them plan tours, target marketing, and understand their audience.

### Negative Impacts

- **Very low pay per stream** - Spotify pays artists an average of approximately £0.003 per stream. An artist would need hundreds of thousands of streams per month to earn a living wage. Only 0.4% of artists on streaming platforms earn enough to make a living from streaming alone.
- **Favours established artists** - The payment model (pro-rata) means the biggest artists take the largest share of the revenue pool. Smaller and independent artists receive a tiny fraction.
- **Decline of the album format** - Streaming encourages singles and playlists over full albums. Artists feel pressure to create "playlist-friendly" tracks rather than cohesive album projects.
- **Playlist gatekeeping** - Getting placed on a major Spotify playlist can make or break an artist's career, giving enormous power to playlist curators and algorithms.
- **Devaluation of music** - When access to all music costs less than a single CD used to, the perceived value of individual songs and albums decreases.
- **Homogenisation** - Algorithms tend to recommend similar-sounding music, potentially narrowing the diversity of what listeners are exposed to and what artists are incentivised to create.
- **UK Parliamentary inquiry** - These issues prompted the UK Parliament's Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Committee to launch an inquiry into the economics of music streaming, resulting in a 120-page report recommending reforms to how artists are compensated.

## Impact of IT Systems on Key Sectors

### Financial Services (Banks, Insurance)

IT systems have transformed financial services dramatically:

- **Online and mobile banking** - Customers can check balances, transfer money, pay bills, set up direct debits, and manage accounts 24/7 from their phones or computers. High-street bank branch numbers have declined significantly as a result. Examples: Barclays app, HSBC online banking.
- **Contactless and digital payments** - Chip-and-PIN, contactless cards, Apple Pay, and Google Pay have replaced cash for many transactions. In 2023, contactless payments accounted for over 50% of all UK transactions.
- **Digital-only banks** - Challenger banks like Monzo, Starling, and Revolut operate entirely through apps with no physical branches, offering real-time spending notifications, budgeting tools, and fee-free international spending.
- **Automated fraud detection** - AI and machine learning systems monitor transactions in real time to flag suspicious activity, such as unusual spending patterns or payments from unfamiliar locations.
- **Insurance comparison and claims** - Comparison websites (CompareTheMarket, GoCompare) use IT systems to instantly compare hundreds of insurance quotes. Claims can be submitted and tracked online. Telematics (black box) insurance uses data from a device in the car to set premiums based on actual driving behaviour.
- **Algorithmic trading** - In investment banking, automated systems execute trades in milliseconds based on market data and algorithms.
- **Open Banking** - APIs allow authorised third-party apps to access bank account data (with customer consent), enabling budgeting apps and services that aggregate accounts from multiple banks.

- **Online courses and MOOCs** - Platforms like Coursera, Udemy, Khan Academy, and the Open University offer courses accessible to anyone with an internet connection, many of them free. This democratises access to education globally.

- **E-books and digital resources** - Students can access textbooks, journals, and research papers online rather than relying on physical libraries.
- **AI tutoring and assessment** - AI-powered tools can provide personalised feedback, adaptive learning paths that adjust difficulty based on student performance, and automated marking of certain assessment types.
- **Record management systems** - Student data, attendance, grades, and timetabling are managed through systems like SIMS (School Information Management System) in schools and UCAS for university applications.
- **Remote and hybrid learning** - Video conferencing (Zoom, Teams) enabled education to continue during lockdowns and now supports flexible learning arrangements.

- **24/7 online news** - News websites and apps (BBC News, The Guardian, Sky News) provide instant access to breaking news at any time, replacing the need to wait for scheduled TV bulletins or the next day's newspaper.

## Impact of IT Systems on Data, Marketing, and Collaboration

### Transactional Data

Transactional data refers to the records generated every time a transaction takes place, such as a purchase, payment, return, or booking.

- **Digital record keeping** - Every online transaction is automatically recorded in a database, creating detailed records of what was bought, when, where, by whom, and how much was paid. This replaces manual paper-based records and reduces human error.
- **Real-time processing** - IT systems process transactions instantly. When a customer buys a product online, the payment is processed, stock levels are updated, and a confirmation email is sent within seconds.
- **Data analysis and trends** - Businesses can analyse transactional data to identify sales trends, peak buying times, popular products, and seasonal patterns. This informs stock ordering, staffing decisions, and pricing strategies.
- **Fraud detection** - Banks and payment processors use transactional data patterns to detect unusual activity. For example, if a card is used in London and then minutes later in another country, the system can flag or block the transaction.
- **Customer insights** - Transactional data builds a picture of individual customer behaviour over time, enabling businesses to understand customer lifetime value, purchase frequency, and average order value.
- **Supply chain management** - Transaction records feed into inventory and supply chain systems, triggering automatic reorders when stock falls below set thresholds.
- **Regulatory compliance** - Businesses must retain transactional records for tax, auditing, and legal purposes. Digital systems make this easier to manage and retrieve than paper records.

### Targeted Marketing

Targeted marketing uses data collected about customers to deliver personalised advertisements and offers to specific groups or individuals, rather than broadcasting the same message to everyone.

- **Data collection** - Businesses collect data through website cookies, purchase history, account information, social media activity, loyalty cards, and app usage to build detailed customer profiles.
- **Customer segmentation** - IT systems group customers into segments based on demographics (age, gender, location), behaviour (what they browse and buy), interests, and spending habits.
- **Personalised advertising** - Social media platforms (Facebook/Meta, Instagram, TikTok) and search engines (Google) allow businesses to target ads at very specific audiences. For example, a sportswear company can target ads at 18-30 year olds who have recently searched for running shoes.
- **Email marketing** - Automated email campaigns send personalised messages based on customer behaviour, such as abandoned basket reminders, product recommendations based on past purchases, or birthday discount codes.
- **Retargeting** - If a customer views a product but does not buy it, ads for that product can follow them across other websites and social media platforms, reminding them to complete the purchase.
- **Loyalty programmes** - Supermarket loyalty cards (e.g. Tesco Clubcard, Nectar) track purchasing habits and offer personalised vouchers and discounts based on what the customer actually buys.
- **A/B testing** - Businesses use IT systems to test different versions of adverts, emails, or web pages to see which performs better, optimising marketing campaigns based on real data.
- **Ethical concerns** - Targeted marketing raises privacy concerns. Customers may feel uncomfortable knowing how much data is collected about them. GDPR requires businesses to get consent for data collection and give customers the right to opt out.

### Collaborative Working

Collaborative working refers to multiple people working together on shared tasks or projects, often from different locations, using IT systems.

- **Cloud-based document editing** - Tools like Google Docs, Microsoft 365, and Notion allow multiple users to edit the same document simultaneously in real time, with changes visible instantly to all collaborators. Version history tracks who changed what and when.
- **Video conferencing** - Platforms like Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet enable face-to-face meetings with participants anywhere in the world. Screen sharing, virtual whiteboards, and recording features enhance collaboration.
- **Instant messaging and channels** - Slack, Microsoft Teams, and Discord provide persistent chat channels organised by topic, project, or team, replacing long email chains with faster, more organised communication.
- **Project management platforms** - Tools like Trello, Asana, and Jira allow teams to assign tasks, set deadlines, track progress, and manage workloads transparently. Everyone can see what needs to be done and who is responsible.
- **Shared cloud storage** - Google Drive, OneDrive, SharePoint, and Dropbox allow teams to store and access files in a central location, eliminating the problem of emailing different versions of files back and forth.
- **Remote and hybrid working** - IT systems have enabled the shift to remote and hybrid work patterns, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Many businesses now allow employees to work from home some or all of the time, supported by collaboration tools.
- **Version control** - In software development, systems like Git and GitHub allow multiple developers to work on the same codebase simultaneously, tracking every change and allowing changes to be merged or reverted.
- **Global collaboration** - IT systems remove geographic barriers, allowing teams spread across different countries and time zones to work together effectively on shared projects.
