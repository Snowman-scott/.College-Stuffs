What is an Acceptable Use Policy

Definition

An Acceptable Use Policy sets rules and guidance for using an organisation s systems, networks, and digital resources.

  • Sets out what users can and cannot do with technology
  • Protects both the organisation and its users
  • Ensures legal compliance and maintains security
  • Common in schools, businesses, libraries, and any setting providing IT access
  • Users usually must accept the AUP before access is granted

Purpose of Acceptable Use Policies

🛡️ Protection

Protects infrastructure from misuse, damage, and threat.

⚖️ Legal compliance

Supports duties under Data Protection, Computer Misuse, and copyright law.

📋 Clarity

Sets expectations for acceptable and unacceptable conduct.

⚠️ Consequences

Explains what will happen if rules are broken.

🔒 Security

Reduces risk of breaches and unauthorised access.

💼 Productivity

Helps ensure resources are used for intended purposes.

What is included in an AUP

  • Internet usage guidance on browsing, downloading, and streaming
  • Email and communication professional standards and anti harassment
  • Password practice strong secrets and keeping credentials safe
  • Software installation approval for programs and apps
  • Data protection handling personal and sensitive data
  • Social media representing the organisation online
  • Copyright and IP using licensed content and respecting creators
  • Monitoring and privacy how activity may be monitored
  • Consequences actions that may follow violations

AUP Do and Do not

✓ Do

  • Use strong unique passwords
  • Log out when stepping away
  • Report incidents promptly
  • Use technology for study or work purposes
  • Respect privacy
  • Follow licensing rules
  • Keep software updated
  • Use approved channels

✗ Do not

  • Share your password
  • Install unauthorised software
  • Access inappropriate content
  • Harass or bully others
  • Bypass security
  • Connect personal devices without permission
  • Share confidential information
  • Engage in illegal activity

Ethics in information technology

What are IT ethics

IT ethics are principles that guide behaviour in the use, development, and management of technology.

  • Ethics go beyond what is legal and consider what is right
  • Balance interests such as privacy and security, innovation and safety
  • New capability can outpace law which creates dilemmas
  • Every user has ethical responsibility

Key ethical principles in IT

Privacy

Respect the right to control personal information.

Example

Do not access files or mail without permission even if you can.

Security

Protect systems, data, and users from unauthorised access and harm.

Example

Use encryption and strong authentication for sensitive data.

Key ethical principles continued

Intellectual property

Respect copyright, patents, and ownership of digital creations.

Example

Do not pirate software or share protected material without permission.

Accuracy and honesty

Ensure information is truthful and not misleading.

Example

Avoid fake accounts, misinformation, and data manipulation.

Further ethical principles

Accessibility

Design for people with diverse abilities and needs.

Example

Provide alt text and support screen reader navigation.

Professional responsibility

Act with integrity and consider wider impact.

Example

Refuse work that would harm others or violate rights.

Common ethical dilemmas

Surveillance and privacy

Employers want to record keystrokes to track productivity. Is this acceptable

Data collection and consent

Apps gather extensive data and users rarely read terms. Is this acceptable

Whistleblowing

Staff discover data is sold without consent. Should this be reported

AI bias

An automated hiring tool disadvantages certain groups. Can it be used

UK legal framework

Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR

Rules for collection, storage, processing, and protection of personal data including subject rights.

Computer Misuse Act 1990

Offences include unauthorised access, malware, and unauthorised modification.

Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988

Protects software and creative works from unauthorised copying and distribution.

Consequences of violations

For individuals

  • Disciplinary action warning, suspension, dismissal
  • Legal outcomes fines and prison for serious offences
  • Loss of access IT privileges removed
  • Reputation damage harm to future opportunity

For organisations

  • Financial penalties large fines for breaches under GDPR
  • Legal liability claims from affected people and regulators
  • Reputation damage loss of trust and business
  • Operational disruption downtime and recovery cost
